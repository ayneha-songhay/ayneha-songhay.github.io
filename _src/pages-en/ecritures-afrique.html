AYNEHA among the writing systems of West Africa

Creating a script of one's own for one's language: West Africa has done it several times over the past two centuries. AYNEHA is part of this history.

A long history

Many African languages were long passed on orally. To write them, people often borrowed an existing alphabet: the Arabic alphabet (Ajami), then the Latin alphabet. But on several occasions, people chose another path: inventing an entirely new script, designed for their language.

Four of these scripts are read, like AYNEHA, from right to left: Mende, N'Ko, Adlam and Dogon NÈNÈ.

A neighbouring script: Dogon NÈNÈ

In Mali, AYNEHA is not alone. Dogon NÈNÈ is a script created by Yahya Sékou Salah GUINDO for the Dogon language and its various varieties: in Dogon, “nènè” means “the language”. Created in 2019, it has 29 letters and is written, like AYNEHA, from right to left. Its letters join together within a word, and accents mark the tones of the language.

Dogon NÈNÈ has its own digits: you can see them and convert your numbers with the site's number converter, alongside AYNEHA, N'Ko and Adlam digits.

More than a tool

A script created for a language does more than a practical job. It also carries a strong symbolic value: it says that this language deserves signs of its own. N'Ko thus went hand in hand with a vast literacy movement and strengthened the cultural identity of speakers of the Manding languages, in Guinea, Mali and beyond.

AYNEHA embraces this double role: writing Songhay precisely, and asserting the graphic independence of the language.

What these scripts teach us

  • A script can succeed when a community learns it, teaches it and passes it on. N'Ko and Adlam are taught today, used on phones and encoded in the Unicode standard: N'Ko since 2006, Adlam since 2016.
  • A script can also fade away. Mende was widely used in the 1920s and 1930s for letters and records, then declined in the face of the Latin alphabet used by literacy campaigns.
  • Tools matter. What cannot be learned easily is not passed on. That is why AYNEHA came from the start with its font, its keyboard, its online tools and an app to learn on one's own.

What sets AYNEHA apart

The creation stories of several of these scripts speak of a dream or a revelation, and some were reserved for initiates. AYNEHA follows another path:

  • A reasoned design: one character for each sound retained, and public, documented writing rules.
  • Open to all: anyone can learn it, free of charge, with no conditions.
  • Free and open: the font is released under the SIL Open Font License 1.1 and the apps' code is free software.
  • Born in the age of the phone: the font, keyboard, apps and website were created together with the script itself.
